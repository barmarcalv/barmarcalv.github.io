## The problem

Wikipedia is not one product. It is a family of sites, maintained partly by staff and
largely by volunteers, rendered in more than 300 languages, read left-to-right and
right-to-left, on hardware that ranges from flagship phones to devices with almost no
memory to spare.

Before Codex, every team solved interface problems on its own. The same button existed
in a dozen versions. Accessibility was a fix applied late, per feature, rather than a
property of the thing itself.

## What we built

Codex is Wikimedia's design system: design tokens, components, patterns and the
documentation that makes them usable by someone who has never met the team that made
them.

Three constraints shaped every decision.

**It has to work in every language.** Not "support internationalization" as a checkbox —
components had to survive German compounds, Japanese line breaking, Arabic and Hebrew
bidirectional layout, and scripts with tall ascenders, without a designer intervening.

**It has to be accessible by default.** Colour contrast, focus order, keyboard paths and
screen reader semantics are built into the components, so a volunteer who uses Codex
gets an accessible result whether or not they know what WCAG is.

**It has to be built in the open.** Contributors are not employees. They cannot be
briefed in a meeting. Every decision had to be documented, argued in public, and
defensible to someone who joined the conversation six months late.

## My part

I worked on the foundations — colour, typography, spacing, iconography and the grid —
and on the components that sit closest to reading and editing. I wrote documentation,
reviewed contributions from outside the team, and spent a good share of the work
explaining decisions rather than making them.

## What I took from it

Designing in the open is slower and better. You cannot ship a shortcut when the
reasoning is public.
